Critical Material Notes

C/SiC + Steam: SiC reacts with H&sub2;O above 1,200°C forming Si(OH)&sub4; gas. ZrO&sub2; TBC and environmental barrier coating (EBC) are essential.
IN718 Limit: Loses strength above 700°C. Use Haynes 230 (max 1,150°C) where needed.
Alkali Seed Corrosion: K/Cs salts corrode some materials. W-Cu and BN are resistant. Ni-superalloy in MHD section needs protective coatings.
CTE Mismatch: C/SiC (~3×10&supmin;&sup6;/K) vs IN718 (~13×10&supmin;&sup6;/K). Use compliant seals (C-seals or ceramic rope). Never bond rigidly.

Print Procedure: IN718 Shells

Plasma, combustion, and nozzle outer shells
  1. Export STL with 0.05 mm chord tolerance, add 0.5 mm stock on mating surfaces
  2. Orient part vertically (axis vertical), add support anchors at overhangs
  3. Load IN718 powder (15–53 μm) in argon atmosphere (<100 ppm O&sub2;)
  4. Build: 40 μm layers, 285 W × 4 lasers, 960 mm/s, 0.11 mm hatch
  5. Wire EDM from build plate
  6. HIP: 1,165°C / 100 MPa / 4 hours in argon
  7. Solution treat: 980°C / 1 hr, air cool
  8. Double age: 720°C/8hr → furnace cool to 620°C/8hr → air cool
  9. CNC machine mating faces to ±0.05 mm
  10. CT scan + FPI + CMM dimensional inspection

Print Procedure: Fuel Injector Ring

Monolithic IN625 with 24 integrated injectors
  1. Design internal manifold with teardrop cross-section (self-supporting)
  2. 24 injector passages with 45° printed swirl vanes
  3. Print flat (ring plane horizontal) on EOS M290
  4. IN625 powder, 40 μm layers, 300 W, 800 mm/s
  5. Stress relief: 870°C / 1 hour
  6. Ream all 24 orifices to 3.00 ±0.02 mm
  7. Flow test: target ±3% uniformity across all injectors
  8. Pressure test manifold: 50 bar hydrostatic (2× operating)
  9. Helium leak check: <1 cc/min at 30 bar
This is the ideal AM candidate — internal channels and integrated features impossible via conventional machining.

  1. Mount intake on assembly fixture — horizontal rail, engine axis horizontal

  4. Mate combustion module — 12× M12 bolts, connect fuel cooling lines
  5. Mate MHD accelerator — 16× M16 bolts (heavier loads), align to ±0.5 mm
  6. Mate nozzle module — 12× M12 bolts, connect EM coil cables + cooling
  7. Install mounting brackets — forward at station 400 mm, aft at station 2,800 mm
  8. Connect all external systems — RF power, fuel, water cooling, LN&sub2;, MHD power buses, sensors, ECU harness
  9. Leak test all fluid circuits — fuel 50 bar He, water 15 bar hydro, cryo vacuum + He
  10. Electrical test — all coils resistance + Hi-pot, all electrodes, all sensors
  11. Weight and CG measurement
  12. Ready for test cell installation

Testing & Validation

Test Sequence

  1. Cold Flow Test — Ambient air through engine. Measure pressure drops, verify mass flow capacity (100 kg/s target), check for leaks.
  2. Plasma Ignition Test — Reduced airflow (10–20%), energize RF at low power, verify plasma, map density vs power.
  3. Combustion Test — Establish plasma, introduce fuel at 10% flow, achieve ignition, increase incrementally to 50% thrust.
  4. MHD Test — Cool magnets, verify field, inject alkali seed, measure gas conductivity, apply accelerator voltage, measure thrust increment.
  5. Full Engine Test — Ramp 25% → 50% → 75% → 100%. Measure thrust on calibrated stand. Record all parameters.
  6. Endurance Run — Sustained operation at rated thrust. Monitor for thermal creep, vibration, seal degradation.

Critical Engineering Challenges

1. Non-Rotating Compression
Without a mechanical compressor, chamber pressure at static conditions is only ~1–3 bar. The engine may require minimum forward velocity (ramjet-like) or an auxiliary boost system for takeoff. This is the single hardest unsolved problem.
2. Electrical Power Demand
MHD acceleration of 80–100 kg/s requires hundreds of kW to MW. The power source (turbogenerator, battery, APU) adds significant weight and complexity. A closed-loop MHD generator/accelerator design could help but adds engineering difficulty.
3. Gas Conductivity
Combustion products at 2,500–3,000 K have electrical conductivity of only 1–5 S/m. Alkali seed (K or Cs, 0.5–1% mass fraction) is required to reach >10 S/m for effective MHD interaction. Seed recovery and environmental impact must be addressed.
4. Thermal Management
2,500–3,200 K gas temperatures for sustained operation demand advanced CMC liners, multi-layer TBC systems, and active cooling. Any cooling failure causes rapid catastrophic damage. Materials life and inspection intervals are critical concerns.

Synergy with HPM-120: A compact fusion reactor shares critical infrastructure with the HPM-120's existing systems: (1) HTS superconducting magnets — the fusion reactor and MHD accelerator both require high-field superconducting coils with cryogenic cooling, allowing a shared cryogenic plant; (2) Plasma physics expertise — the same RF/microwave plasma technology used in the ionization chamber applies to plasma heating in fusion; (3) MHD power conversion — fusion plasma exhaust could be passed through an MHD generator channel to directly extract electrical power, bypassing thermal cycles entirely. Integrated Cryogenic & Thermal Management System

Yes, cryogenic coolant is required through the engine. The HPM-120 operates across an extreme temperature range: from 77 K (liquid nitrogen for superconducting magnets) to 3,200 K (plasma combustion). A multi-circuit cooling system is essential to maintain structural integrity and prevent thermal runaway in every section.

Why LN&sub2; and not liquid helium? The NbTi superconducting coils in the MHD section have a critical temperature of 9.2 K, which normally requires LHe (4.2 K). However, by using high-current-density coils and limiting the operating current to 80% of critical, LN&sub2; cooling at 77 K with a secondary cryocooler stage can bring the coil temperature down to 4–10 K locally. Alternatively, upgrading to HTS (REBCO) magnets with T_c > 90 K allows full LN&sub2; operation, eliminating liquid helium entirely. HTS upgrade is strongly recommended for flight applications.

Critical Thermal Risks & Mitigations

Cryogenic ↔ Hot Section Interface
The MHD section has 77 K magnets separated by only 200 mm from 3,000 K gas. The cryostat vacuum gap and MLI insulation must maintain <50 W heat leak. Any vacuum breach causes immediate magnet quench (superconductor loses superconductivity), resulting in rapid boil-off and potential structural damage from thermal shock. Mitigation: Redundant vacuum monitoring, automatic LN&sub2; dump valve, quench detection on every coil.
Combustion Cooling Channel Blockage
If any of the 48 fuel cooling channels become blocked (by coke, debris, or manufacturing defect), local wall temperature will exceed 1,800 K within seconds, causing C/SiC liner failure and burn-through. Mitigation: 10 μm fuel filter, thermal stability monitoring per channel (thermocouples), oversized channels (4×3 mm), and CT-scan inspection of all printed cooling passages before assembly.
Thermal Expansion Mismatch
C/SiC liners (CTE: ~3×10&supmin;&sup6;/K) expand much less than Inconel 718 shells (CTE: ~13×10&supmin;&sup6;/K). At operating temperature, the outer shell grows ~10 mm more in diameter than the liner. Mitigation: Compliant mounting (C-seals, spring clips, ceramic rope gaskets). Never bond rigidly. Design 3–5 mm radial clearance at room temperature.
Transient Thermal Shock (Start-up / Shutdown)
Rapid heat-up from 288 K to 3,200 K generates severe thermal gradients. C/SiC handles this well (low CTE, high thermal shock resistance), but Inconel and TBC coatings are vulnerable to spallation. Mitigation: Controlled ramp rate: 100 K/s max through combustion zone. RF plasma at low power before fuel introduction to pre-heat gradually.
